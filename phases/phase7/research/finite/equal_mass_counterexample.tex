\section{Equal source masses do not control hierarchy price for smooth losses}
\label{sec:equal-mass-generator}

For a convex generator $\phi$ and a finite source with binary posteriors
$\eta_x$, define
\[
 D_\phi(\mathcal P)=\sum_xp_x\phi(\eta_x)
  -\sum_{C\in\mathcal P}p_C\phi(\bar\eta_C),\qquad
 \bar\eta_C=p_C^{-1}\sum_{x\in C}p_x\eta_x.
\]
Let $D_{\phi,k}^*$ be its flat $k$-cell minimum.  A deterministic
four-state hierarchy is a nested pair $(\mathcal H_2,\mathcal H_3)$
with exactly two and three cells; the one-cell and singleton levels can
always be appended.  Its minimax excess price is
\[
 \rho_{\phi,\mathrm{det}}
 =\min_{\mathcal H}\max_{k=2,3}
     D_\phi(\mathcal H_k)/D_{\phi,k}^*.
\]
For a channel $X\to Z$, set
$D_\phi(Z)=\mathbb E\phi(\eta_X)
 -\mathbb E\phi(\mathbb E[\eta_X\mid Z])$.
The stochastic price takes the infimum of the same maximum over
$Y-X-Z_3-Z_2$ with output budgets three and two.  No public randomization
variable is supplied to the predictor.  All denominators below are positive.

\begin{theorem}[A fixed smooth generator with an equal-mass obstruction]
\label{thm:equal-mass-smooth}
There exists a single symmetric function $\phi\in C^\infty([0,1])$
with $0\leq\phi''\leq1$, $\phi''>0$ on $(0,1)$, and finite endpoint
derivatives, for which the following holds.  For every integer $j\geq16$
there is a full-support four-state binary-target law with
$p_A=p_C=p_D=p_B=1/4$ such that
\[
 \rho_{\phi,\mathrm{det},j}
   =\frac{j^2-j+8}{2j+4}
   =\frac j2-\frac32+\frac7{j+2},\qquad
 \rho_{\phi,\mathrm{stoch},j}\sim\frac j4.
 \tag{8}\label{eq:equal-mass-prices}
\]
The generator is strictly convex, while all its derivatives of order at
least two vanish at zero and one.  Its associated strictly proper binary
losses can be smooth and bounded in $[0,1]$.
\end{theorem}

Thus a positive source-mass bound does not by itself control the excess
hierarchy price uniformly across fixed smooth bounded-slope generators.
The logarithmic source-mass bound relies on properties of logarithmic
loss.  The theorem does not assert an unbounded total-risk price.

\subsection{A smooth four-point gadget with exact costs}

Fix an even nonnegative $C^\infty$ density $\kappa$ supported in
$[-1,1]$, with integral one, and write
$\kappa_\eta(u)=\eta^{-1}\kappa(u/\eta)$.  Define a smoothed hinge
\[
 H_\eta(s)=\int_{\mathbb R}(s-u)_+\kappa_\eta(u)\,du.
\]
It is convex and smooth, with $H_\eta''=\kappa_\eta$.
Symmetry gives zero mean for the kernel, so
$H_\eta(s)=0$ for $s\leq-\eta$ and $H_\eta(s)=s$ for $s\geq\eta$.
For $0<\varepsilon\leq1/16$, take $\eta=\varepsilon/4$ and put
\[
 F_\varepsilon(z)
 =H_\eta(-z-\varepsilon)
  +H_\eta(z-1-\varepsilon)
  +4\varepsilon^2(z-1/2)^2.
 \tag{9}\label{eq:hinge-gadget}
\]
It is symmetric about $1/2$ and has $F_\varepsilon''\geq8\varepsilon^2$.
For this intermediate calculation use the four real locations
\[
 (z_A,z_C,z_D,z_B)=(-1,0,1,2),\qquad p_x=1/4.
\]
These locations will later be transformed to valid binary posteriors.

\begin{lemma}[Exact gadget costs and deterministic optimum]
\label{lem:equal-mass-costs}
Let $a,b,r,q,t,u$ denote, respectively, a near pair $AC$ or $DB$, a
far pair $AD$ or $CB$, the middle pair $CD$, the outer pair $AB$, a
triple with one outer point, and a triple with both outer points.
Their Jensen costs for (9) are
\[
\begin{array}{lll}
 a=\varepsilon/4+\varepsilon^2/2,&
 r=\varepsilon^2/2,&
 b=t=(1-\varepsilon)/4+2\varepsilon^2,\\[2pt]
 q=(1-\varepsilon)/2+9\varepsilon^2/2,&
 u=(1-\varepsilon)/2+14\varepsilon^2/3.&
\end{array}
\tag{10}\label{eq:gadget-six-costs}
\]
The unique flat optima are $AC\mid DB$ and $A\mid CD\mid B$, with
costs $2a$ and $r$.  The deterministic price is
\[
 \min\{\alpha,\beta\}=\beta,
 \quad \alpha=\frac ar=\frac1{2\varepsilon}+1,\quad
 \beta=\frac t{2a}
       =\frac{1-\varepsilon+8\varepsilon^2}
              {2\varepsilon+4\varepsilon^2}.
 \tag{11}\label{eq:gadget-price}
\]
Every deterministic risk-ratio vector is coordinatewise at least one
of $(1,\alpha)$ and $(\beta,1)$, and both vectors are attained.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The possible means of nonempty subsets of the four equally weighted
locations belong to
\[
 \{-1,-1/2,0,1/3,1/2,2/3,1,3/2,2\}.
\]
Each lies outside the smoothing bands of width $\eta$ around
$-\varepsilon$ and $1+\varepsilon$.  At all points used in any
partition cost, the hinge part of (9) consequently agrees exactly with
\[
 V_\varepsilon(z)=(-z-\varepsilon)_+
                         +(z-1-\varepsilon)_+.
\]
Its values at the four inputs are $(1-\varepsilon,0,0,1-\varepsilon)$.
The near-pair mean is $-1/2$ or $3/2$, and its hinge cost is
$(1-\varepsilon)/4-(1/2)(1/2-\varepsilon)=\varepsilon/4$.
The middle-pair mean is $1/2$ and its hinge cost is zero.
The far-pair and one-outer-point triple means are zero or one; their
hinge costs are $(1-\varepsilon)/4$.  The outer-pair mean is $1/2$,
and the two-outer-point triple mean is $1/3$ or $2/3$; their hinge costs
are $(1-\varepsilon)/2$.

The Jensen cost of $(z-1/2)^2$ equals weighted within-cell variance.
For these six cell types its values are respectively
$1/8,1/2,1/8,9/8,1/2,7/6$, in the order $a,b,r,q,t,u$.
Multiplication by $4\varepsilon^2$ proves (10).

There are seven two-cell partitions, with costs
$2a,2b,q+r,t,t,u,u$, and six three-cell partitions, with costs
$a,a,b,b,r,q$.  For $\varepsilon\leq1/16$ one has
$r<a<b$, $q>a$, $2a<t$, and $t<q+r,u$.
These strict inequalities identify the unique flat minima.

If the fine partition merges $CD$, its possible coarse costs are
$t,t,q+r$, so $(\beta,1)$ is its best ratio vector.  If the fine
partition merges a near pair, the coarse optimum $AC\mid DB$ is
available, attaining $(1,\alpha)$.  Any other fine pair has cost
at least $a$, so its vector is dominated by $(1,\alpha)$.
This proves the Pareto assertion and the minimum formula.  Finally
\[
 \alpha-\beta
 =\frac{5\varepsilon-4\varepsilon^2}
          {2\varepsilon+4\varepsilon^2}>0,
\]
proving the exact value $\beta$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Stochastic refinement of the gadget}

\begin{lemma}[A sharp leading stochastic price]
\label{lem:equal-mass-stochastic}
For the gadget (9), under the same two output budgets,
\[
 1+\frac{(\alpha-1)(\beta-1)}{\alpha+\beta-2}
 \leq\rho_{\varepsilon,\mathrm{stoch}}
 \leq\frac1{4\varepsilon}+\frac43.
 \tag{12}\label{eq:gadget-stochastic-bracket}
\]
In particular $\rho_{\varepsilon,\mathrm{stoch}}
\sim1/(4\varepsilon)$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
For any distribution on deterministic hierarchies, the Pareto assertion
reduces minimization of the maximum expected ratio to a mixture of
$(1,\alpha)$ and $(\beta,1)$.  Equalizing its two coordinates gives
the left side of (12).

To apply this to arbitrary stochastic chains, represent the fine
encoder and its coarsener by independent random function tables,
independent of the source.  Conditional on the tables $U$, the two
representations are deterministic and nested.  Convexity gives
\[
 D_{F_\varepsilon}(Z_k)
 \geq\mathbb E_U D_{F_\varepsilon}(Z_k\mid U).
\]
Indeed, revealing $U$ makes the conditional mean more variable, so
Jensen's inequality increases the expectation of $F_\varepsilon$ being
subtracted from the fixed full-information term.  For each fixed table,
any partitions with fewer than the respective budgets can be refined
to exactly three and two cells while retaining nesting: first split the
fine cells to three, keeping each inside its original coarse cell.
Retain the coarse partition if it already has two cells; if it has only
one, group the three fine cells into two nonempty coarse cells.
Refinement decreases Jensen cost.  Hence a distribution
on exact deterministic hierarchies has expected coordinate costs no
greater than those of the stochastic chain.  This proves the lower
bound without giving the predictor a free randomization label.

For the upper bound, use fine outputs $0,m,1$ with
\[
 A\mapsto0,\qquad C\mapsto m,\qquad
 D\mapsto\tfrac12 m+\tfrac12 1,\qquad B\mapsto1,
\]
and coarsen $0,m$ to one output.  Here the displayed mixture for $D$
means an independent random choice between the two indicated outputs.
At the fine level, the middle cell has mass $3/8$ and location mean
$1/3$; the right cell has mass $3/8$ and mean $5/3$.
At the coarse level, the left cell has mass $5/8$ and mean $-1/5$.
All these means are outside the smoothing bands when
$\varepsilon\leq1/16$.  Direct evaluation gives
\[
 D_{F_\varepsilon}(Z_3)
   =\varepsilon/8+(2/3)\varepsilon^2,\qquad
 D_{F_\varepsilon}(Z_2)
   =1/8+\varepsilon/2+(26/15)\varepsilon^2.
 \tag{13}\label{eq:gadget-stochastic-costs}
\]
For detail, the fine hinge cost is $\varepsilon/8$, and the sum of
fine weighted variances is $1/12+1/12=1/6$.
The coarse hinge cost is
$(1/8+3\varepsilon/8)+\varepsilon/8$; its sum of variances is
$7/20+1/12=13/30$.  Multiplying variances by $4\varepsilon^2$
proves (13).  The fine ratio equals $1/(4\varepsilon)+4/3$.
The coarse cost minus this ratio times $2a$ is
$-5\varepsilon/12+2\varepsilon^2/5<0$.
This proves the upper bound.  Since both $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are
asymptotic to $1/(2\varepsilon)$, the lower bound has the same
leading coefficient, completing the proof.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Embedding all gadgets into one smooth generator}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:equal-mass-smooth}]
For each integer $j\geq16$, let
\[
 a_j=2^{-j-2},\quad l_j=2^{-j-3},\quad
 I_j=(a_j,a_j+l_j),\quad\lambda_j=e^{-j^2}.
\]
These intervals are pairwise disjoint and accumulate only at zero.
Choose a fixed smooth cutoff $\chi:\mathbb R\to[0,1]$ equal to one
on $[1/8,7/8]$ and zero outside $(1/16,15/16)$.
Use the symmetric positive baseline
\[
 b(x)=e^{-1/[x(1-x)]}\quad(0<x<1),\qquad b(0)=b(1)=0.
\]
For $x\in I_j$, put $t=(x-a_j)/l_j$ and define
\[
 g(x)=(1-\chi(t))b(x)
    +\chi(t)\frac{16\lambda_j}{l_j^2}
       F_{1/j}''(4t-3/2).
 \tag{14}\label{eq:equal-mass-gluing}
\]
On the reflected intervals set $g(x)=g(1-x)$, and elsewhere let $g=b$.
Every component is positive at interior points, because
$F_{1/j}''\geq8/j^2$ and $b>0$.  The cutoff vanishes on neighborhoods
of interval boundaries, so the definitions match smoothly there.

For each fixed derivative order $m$, the derivatives of the kernel
give $\|F_{1/j}^{(m+2)}\|_\infty\leq C_m j^{m+1}+8/j^2$.
The constant second derivative term need only be included for $m=0$;
the displayed larger bound is valid in every case.  Consequently
the $m$th derivative of the second summand in (14) is bounded by
\[
 C_m\lambda_j l_j^{-m-2}(1+j^{m+1})
 \leq C_m e^{-j^2+C_mj}.
 \tag{15}\label{eq:equal-target-flatness}
\]
The baseline and cutoff term has corresponding bound
$C_me^{-c2^j+C_mj}$: derivatives of $b$ are $b$ times rational
functions with poles at the endpoints, while derivatives of the
rescaled fixed cutoff contribute only powers of $l_j^{-1}$.
Both estimates vanish faster than $a_j^N$ for every fixed $m,N$.
Away from the patches, the baseline has the same flatness at zero.
Thus each derivative of $g$ tends to zero faster than any power of
$x$ as $x\downarrow0$.  Defining all endpoint derivatives to be zero
is consistent with differentiation, since $g^{(m)}(x)/x\to0$;
induction proves $g\in C^\infty([0,1])$.  Reflection gives the same
conclusion at one.

Set $M=\max_{[0,1]}g>0$ and define
\[
 \phi''=g/M,\qquad\phi(1/2)=\phi'(1/2)=0.
\]
This function is symmetric, smooth on the closed interval, and strictly
convex because its second derivative is strictly positive in the
interior.  It has finite endpoint slopes and all derivatives of order
at least two vanish at the endpoints.  By normalization
$0\leq\phi''\leq1$.

On $J_j=[a_j+l_j/8,a_j+7l_j/8]$, (14) implies that
\[
 \phi(x)=\frac{\lambda_j}{M}
  F_{1/j}\left(4\frac{x-a_j}{l_j}-\frac32\right)+A_jx+B_j
 \tag{16}\label{eq:equal-local-identity}
\]
for suitable affine constants.  Now put equal source masses $1/4$
at the four binary posteriors
\[
 (\eta_A,\eta_C,\eta_D,\eta_B)
 =a_j+l_j(1/8,3/8,5/8,7/8).
 \tag{17}\label{eq:equal-posteriors}
\]
They are distinct, strictly between zero and one, and yield a full-support
joint law.  Under the coordinate transformation in (16) they correspond
to $(-1,0,1,2)$.  Every deterministic cell mean and every stochastic
encoded posterior stays in $J_j$.  Affine cancellation therefore proves
the exact identities
\[
 D_\phi(\mathcal P)=\frac{\lambda_j}{M}D_{F_{1/j}}(\mathcal P),
 \qquad D_\phi(Z)=\frac{\lambda_j}{M}D_{F_{1/j}}(Z)
 \tag{18}\label{eq:equal-all-encoder-identity}
\]
for all partitions and all encoders.  In particular the flat denominators
are $(\lambda_j/M)2a>0$ and $(\lambda_j/M)r>0$.
Lemma~\ref{lem:equal-mass-costs} with $\varepsilon=1/j$ gives the
exact deterministic expression (8), and
Lemma~\ref{lem:equal-mass-stochastic} gives the stochastic asymptotic.

To obtain the stated bounded proper loss, write
$h(q)=\phi(0)-\phi(q)$ and set
\[
 \ell(q,1)=h(q)+(1-q)h'(q),\qquad
 \ell(q,0)=h(q)-qh'(q).
\]
Their expected regret is the Bregman divergence generated by $\phi$,
so strict convexity proves strict propriety.  Endpoint supporting lines
make the losses nonnegative.  They are smooth, with derivatives
$\ell(q,1)'=-(1-q)\phi''(q)$ and
$\ell(q,0)'=q\phi''(q)$.  Their largest values equal
$-\phi'(0)=\phi'(1)=\int_0^{1/2}\phi''(t)dt\leq1/2$.
They hence lie in $[0,1]$.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Total-risk limitation.}
The construction deliberately establishes an excess-risk boundary.
For the displayed proper loss let $c=-\phi'(0)>0$.  Since
$h(q)=cq+o(q)$ as $q\downarrow0$ and all posteriors (17) lie between
$a_j$ and $3a_j/2$, their full-information Bayes risk $B_j$ is at least
$ca_j/2$ eventually.  On $[-1,2]$, the convex nonnegative gadget
$F_{1/j}$ is bounded above by its equal endpoint values
$1-1/j+9/j^2<2$.  Equation (18) therefore gives
$D_\phi(Z)\leq2\lambda_j/M$ for every encoder.  All total-risk ratios
to the respective flat total-risk optima are at most
\[
 1+\frac{4\lambda_j}{Mca_j}=1+o(1).
\]
Thus total-risk hierarchy prices for these very instances tend to one.
The result does not show large absolute excess loss: the flat distortions
and all copied costs are extremely small.

\paragraph{Scope and attribution.}
Smooth cutoffs, proper-score representations, and Jensen/Bregman
centroid identities are classical.  The ingredients new to this research
candidate are the exact equal-mass hinge-gadget Pareto costs, its
stochastic leading coefficient, and its embedding into one fixed smooth
generator.  Priority is not asserted; the accompanying primary-source
audit distinguishes this statement from greedy Bregman clustering and
from uniform curvature comparisons.  This argument does not determine
the universal sharp logarithmic-loss coefficient, the all-capacity
price for larger sources, or a classification of all generators.
